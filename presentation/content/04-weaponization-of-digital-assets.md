# Weaponization of Digital Assets

## The Russian Method

The first form of **weaponization of digital assets** discussed is the **Russian method**, defined as:

> Turning digital tools, platforms, technologies, or content into **instruments used for harm, manipulation, or warfare**.

More broadly, this involves using digital resources in aggressive, harmful, or deceptive ways to advance a particular agenda, cause damage, or gain strategic advantage. The key tactics include:

- **Cyberwarfare** -- state-sponsored attacks on critical infrastructure and government systems
- **Disinformation campaigns** -- coordinated efforts to spread false or misleading information through digital channels
- **Ransomware attacks** -- encrypting victims' data and demanding payment for its release
- **Digital extortion and blackmail** -- leveraging stolen data or compromised systems for coercive purposes
- **Financial manipulation** -- using digital means to disrupt financial systems or markets

## Hacker Mercenaries Working for Autocratic Governments

The suspicion of Russian **hackers interfering in foreign affairs** is widespread and well-documented, particularly concerning **elections and geopolitical events**. This topic remains a major concern in international cybersecurity and politics.

Notable instances of suspected interference include:

- **Troll farms** -- organized operations producing and spreading propaganda and disinformation at scale
- **Ukraine (2014 to 2025)** -- sustained cyber operations accompanying the military conflict
- **US presidential elections (2016)** -- widely reported hacking and influence operations
- **French presidential elections (2017)** -- attempted interference in the democratic process
- **European elections (2024)** -- ongoing concerns about digital manipulation of EU-wide votes
- **Romania presidential elections (2025)** -- the most recent case of suspected interference

## On the Trail of Russian Hackers

The presentation references the investigative work of **Etienne Huver**, a journalist from **Slug News** and winner of the **Prix Albert-Londres 2016** (one of the most prestigious journalism awards in the French-speaking world).

Huver investigated cybercriminals suspected of working for **Vladimir Putin**, covering events from hacking into **US Democratic Party e-mails** to **power cuts in Ukraine**.

His work is documented in:

- **Book:** *"Les Nouvelles Guerres: Sur la piste des hackers russes"* ("The New Wars: On the Trail of Russian Hackers") by Etienne Huver and Boris Razon, published by Arte Editions/Stock
- **Arte Documentary:** *"Les nouveaux mercenaires russes"* ("The New Russian Mercenaries")
- **France 24:** *"Hackers russes: le numerique comme arme de guerre"* ("Russian Hackers: Digital Technology as a Weapon of War")
- **France Inter:** *"L'instant M -- Les hackers russes: nouvelles armes de guerre"* ("Russian Hackers: New Weapons of War")

## The US Method

The second form of weaponization presented is the **US method**, which shares the same broad definition:

> Turning **digital tools**, **platforms**, **technologies**, or **content** into instruments used for **harm**, **manipulation**, or **warfare**.

However, the US method operates through a different mechanism:

> Disrupting your operations by requesting from **hyperscalers** hosting the services supporting your infrastructure to **stop delivering them**.

This refers to the risk that **US-based cloud providers** (such as AWS, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud) could be compelled by the US government to cut off services to foreign entities -- whether countries, companies, or organizations. Because much of Europe's digital infrastructure runs on American hyperscaler platforms, this represents a form of **technological leverage** that could be exercised as a geopolitical weapon without firing a single shot or writing a single line of malicious code.

Unlike the Russian method, which relies on covert offensive operations, the US method leverages **legitimate business relationships and market dominance** to exert pressure -- making it arguably more insidious because the dependency is built into the fabric of everyday digital operations.
